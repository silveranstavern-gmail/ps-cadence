The Clockmaker's Apprentice



Rain had followed Teodor all the way down Lantern Street, and by the time he reached the shop at No. 14 his collar was soaked through.



The sign above the door still read "Halvard & Daughter, Clocks Repaired Since 1872," though the paint on the ampersand had flaked into something that looked more like a question mark.



He pushed the door open. A small brass bell announced him.



"You're late," said a voice from the back of the shop.



"Seven minutes," Teodor said. "The tram stopped twice on the bridge."



"Seven minutes is four hundred and twenty seconds." Maren Halvard stepped out from between two grandfather clocks, wiping her hands on a cloth. "In this building, that's an eternity."



She did not sound angry. She rarely did. She simply said true things in a flat voice and let them sit in the air until you felt them.



Teodor hung his coat on the hook by the stove and looked around.



Every wall was covered. Cuckoo clocks with carved pine birds. Railway clocks with plain black numerals. A ship's chronometer in a mahogany box, its lid propped open like a mouth waiting to speak. Somewhere among them, something ticked half a beat slower than everything else.



He had noticed it the first day. He noticed it every day.



"The slow one," he said. "You still haven't found it?"



Maren's mouth tightened at the corner. "I haven't been looking."



"You've been looking for three weeks."



"Then I've been looking very carefully." She handed him a tray of tiny screws, each sorted by size into a little paper cup. "Number two screwdriver. The Fenwick carriage clock on the bench needs a new mainspring, and Mrs. Okafor wants it back by Thursday."



"Which Thursday?"



"This one."



Teodor sighed and sat down at the bench.



The Fenwick clock was beautiful in the way that old, slightly ridiculous things are beautiful. Its case was gilded brass, its face painted with a tiny ship sailing across a sea that had faded from blue to the color of weak tea. When he opened the back, the smell of old oil drifted out, sweet and metallic.



He worked for an hour without speaking.



Outside, the rain thinned to a drizzle and then to nothing. A delivery cart rattled past. Someone laughed on the far side of the street, and the sound came through the glass softened, as if from another year.



"Teodor."



He looked up. Maren was standing in the middle of the shop with her head tilted, listening.



"Do you hear that?"



He listened. Two hundred clocks ticking, more or less together. And under them, the slow one: tick... tick... a fraction late each time, like a person who keeps almost catching up in a conversation.



"It's louder," he said.



"It's closer." She turned slowly in place. "It moved."



"Clocks don't move."



"This one does." She said it the way she said everything, flatly, as though she were reading the price off a tag. Then she crossed to the back wall and put her hand flat against the plaster beside the ship's chronometer.



"Here," she said. "It's inside the wall."



Teodor set down his screwdriver. "Maren, that's an exterior wall. There's nothing on the other side but the alley."



"I know what's on the other side." She knocked twice. The sound was hollow.



They looked at each other.



It took them twenty minutes, a flat-head chisel, and a great deal of plaster dust to open a hole the size of a dinner plate. Behind it was a narrow cavity, perhaps eight inches deep, and inside the cavity, wrapped in a strip of faded green velvet, was a pocket watch.



Maren lifted it out with both hands.



It was silver, or had been once. The case was tarnished almost black, and the crystal was scratched in a fine web of lines. But it was running. Tick... tick... half a beat slow, exactly as it had been for three weeks. For longer, probably. For much longer.



"Who would wall up a watch?" Teodor asked.



Maren turned it over. There was an engraving on the back, worn shallow by years of thumbs: To M. H. — Keep time for me until I return. — A. H., 14 March 1919.



She was quiet for a long moment.



"My great-grandmother was named Margit," she said finally. "Margit Halvard. Her husband, Anders, went north for work the winter after the war. The story was that he never came back."



"The story was?"



"The story was," she repeated. "Nobody ever said what happened. Only that she kept the shop open and raised my grandfather alone, and that she was very strict about clocks running on time." Something moved across her face that might, in another person, have been a smile. "I always assumed that was where I got it from."



Teodor looked at the watch, still ticking in her palm.



"It's been running for over a hundred years," he said. "That's not possible. Nobody's wound it."



"No," Maren agreed. "It isn't possible."



She opened the case. Inside, the movement was clean, almost bright, as though someone had serviced it last week. A tiny folded square of paper was tucked behind the balance wheel. Maren drew it out with tweezers and unfolded it on the bench under the lamp.



The handwriting was small and careful. Teodor read it over her shoulder.



If you are reading this, then you heard it too. That means the shop is in good hands. Wind nothing, fix nothing. It does not need you. It only needs someone to listen. — M.



The watch ticked. Tick... tick...



And then, very slowly, over the course of perhaps a minute, it caught up. The half-beat closed. The slow tick slid into place among the others until Teodor could no longer pick it out at all. Two hundred clocks, and one more, all keeping the same time.



Maren closed the case.



"Well," she said, in her flat, ordinary voice. "That's one problem solved."



She set the watch on the shelf beside the ship's chronometer, facing out, where anyone who came into the shop would see it. Then she brushed the plaster dust from her sleeves and looked pointedly at the Fenwick clock.



"Thursday," she said.



"This Thursday," Teodor said.



"This Thursday."



He picked up the number two screwdriver and went back to work. Outside, the clouds had begun to break, and a thin bar of late sunlight fell through the front window and across the floor, reaching almost, but not quite, to the bench.
